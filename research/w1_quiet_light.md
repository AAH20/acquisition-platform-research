## Executive Summary

Quiet Light Brokerage (QLB), founded in 2006 by Mark Daoust in Mooresville, North Carolina, is one of the earliest and most recognized boutique brokerages specializing exclusively in online business acquisitions. With over 750 businesses sold and more than $500 million in total transaction value, QLB has established itself as a premium, advisor-led brokerage serving the $100K–$25M deal range. Its core differentiator is a team of advisors who are all former online business operators—entrepreneurs who have personally built, bought, and sold internet businesses. The firm handles approximately 100–150 deals per year, with a significant concentration between $500K and $3M, and claims that 85% of listings sell within 90 days or less.

## 1. Business Model

### 1.1 Core Positioning

Quiet Light operates as a **boutique advisory brokerage**, not a marketplace. Unlike platforms such as Flippa (1,000+ active listings) or Empire Flippers (200+ listings monthly), QLB handles roughly 100–150 deals per year. This lower volume is intentional, reflecting a thesis that fewer, better-vetted deals produce better outcomes for both buyers and sellers.

QLB's model emphasizes **relationship over volume**. Each seller is represented by a dedicated advisor who has personally built and sold businesses in that category. The firm does not list physical retail stores, brick-and-mortar service businesses, or traditional franchises—its exclusive focus is internet-based businesses including SaaS, e-commerce, Amazon FBA, content sites, lead generation businesses, agencies, and membership sites.

### 1.2 Revenue Model

QLB operates on a **traditional broker commission model**, not a marketplace listing fee model. There are no upfront listing fees, no retainers, and no membership costs. The brokerage's revenue comes exclusively from a success fee paid at closing by the seller. This structure aligns incentives: QLB only gets paid when the deal closes.

### 1.3 Organizational Structure

The firm employs approximately 39 people (per Tracxn data), with a team of 8–18 advisors depending on the source. The founding story is notable: in 2008, Mark Daoust hired proven salespeople to work with sellers, but the results were underwhelming. It wasn't until 2010 that the firm discovered its winning formula—hiring entrepreneurs who had bought, sold, and started significant online businesses. Many current advisors started as QLB clients themselves.

## 2. Fees and Commission Structure

### 2.1 Commission Schedule

QLB uses a **sliding-scale commission** that decreases as deal size increases. The exact schedule varies slightly across sources, but the consensus structure is:

| Sale Price Range | Commission Rate |
|-----------------|----------------|
| Up to $1M | 10–15% |
| $1M – $2M | 9% |
| $2M – $3M | 8% |
| $3M – $4M | 7% |
| $4M – $5M | 6% |
| $5M – $6M | 5% |
| $6M – $7M | 4% |
| $7M+ | 3% |

Some sources cite a simpler structure: 8% on deals $0–$1M, 7.5% on $1M–$2M, and 7% above $2M. The variation likely reflects negotiated rates and different time periods. QLB does not publicly publish its fee schedule; rates are individually negotiated per engagement based on size and complexity.

### 2.2 Buyer Fees

**Buyers pay $0 to QLB.** There is no buyer commission, no membership fee, and no upfront cost. The seller's commission is factored into the asking price. However, buyers do bear their own due diligence costs:

- Legal review: $3,000–$8,000
- Accountant review: $1,500–$4,000
- Technical audits (for SaaS): $2,000–$10,000
- Escrow and legal: ~$5,000 total on a standard deal

### 2.3 Commission Comparison

| Platform | Commission | Listing Fee | Total on $500K Sale |
|----------|-----------|-------------|---------------------|
| Quiet Light | 10–15% sliding | $0 | ~$60,000 |
| Empire Flippers | 15% blended | $0 | ~$75,000 |
| FE International | 10–15% | $0 | ~$75,000 |
| Flippa | 5–10% | $499 | ~$25,149 |
| ExitBid | 0% | $199 flat | $199 |

QLL's commission is competitive on larger deals. At the $5M range, the sliding scale (5–6%) can result in meaningfully lower fees than Empire Flippers' fixed blended rate. However, on smaller deals (under $200K), the 10–15% commission represents a substantial absolute cost.

### 2.4 Commission Justification

QLB argues that its higher commission rate is offset by:
- Faster sales (85% within 90 days)
- Higher final prices (47% of listings sell above asking price)
- Greater certainty of closing (85–90% of signed LOIs close, vs. 60–70% on open marketplaces)
- Reduced legal risk in complex deals ($50K–$200K in potential dispute costs)

## 3. Vetting Process

### 3.1 Seller Vetting

QLB's vetting is the single biggest factor separating it from competitors. The firm accepts approximately **10–15% of inbound seller inquiries**, meaning 85–90% of sellers who approach them are turned away. This creates artificial scarcity that benefits accepted sellers through credibility signaling and reduced marketplace saturation.

**Vetting criteria include:**

1. **Revenue consistency:** 12+ months of stable revenue with minimal month-to-month swings. Erratic income patterns, seasonal spikes, one-time events, and recent launches are rejected. QLB prefers "boring consistency" over exciting growth trajectories.

2. **Profit margin thresholds:** Target profit margins are 50%+. Businesses with 30–40% margins face scrutiny about sustainability and scalability.

3. **Traffic diversification:** Heavy reliance on Google (80%+ of traffic from organic search) triggers concerns. QLB prefers businesses with email lists, direct traffic, social audiences, or paid acquisition channels.

4. **Clean financial records:** P&L statements must be clear, accurate, and supported by bank statements, merchant account statements, and tax returns. Sellers without organized financials are rejected or required to spend months cleaning records before reapplying.

5. **Transferability assessment:** If the business relies on the seller's personal brand, network, or skills, transferability is limited. QLB evaluates whether buyers can maintain performance post-acquisition.

6. **Growth trajectory:** Flat revenue with high margins outranks 30% growth with thin margins. QLB is selling income streams, not potential.

### 3.2 Buyer Vetting

Buyers must also qualify before accessing listings:

1. **Initial qualification call:** 20–30 minute call covering background, capital availability, acquisition thesis, and timeline.
2. **Financial qualification:** Proof of liquid capital and business experience.
3. **NDA requirement:** Full financials and seller identity are behind NDA. Summary metrics (revenue, profit, multiple, asset type) are publicly visible without NDA.
4. **Proof of funds:** Required before accessing detailed listings.

## 4. Valuation Methodology

### 4.1 Valuation Framework

QLB uses a proprietary **Four Pillars of Value** framework to evaluate businesses:

1. **Growth:** Revenue trends, traffic trends, market opportunity
2. **Risk:** Revenue concentration, platform dependency, competitive threats
3. **Transferability:** Owner dependency, team structure, process documentation
4. **Documentation:** Financial records, analytics, SOPs

This framework shapes the entire seller experience—from initial consultation through listing preparation. Sellers receive specific, actionable feedback on where to improve before listing.

### 4.2 Valuation Multiples

QLB averages **40–48x monthly profit** (SDE multiples), which is:
- 10–20% higher than Empire Flippers (35–42x)
- 50–100% higher than Flippa (20–30x)

**Multiples by asset type:**

| Asset Type | Multiple Range |
|-----------|---------------|
| SaaS / Software | 48–60x |
| E-commerce | 40–48x |
| Content sites | 38–45x |
| Amazon FBA | 35–42x |

**Adjustments:**
- High-growth businesses (20%+ YoY): +5–10x to base multiples
- Declining businesses: −10–15x
- Niche liquidity and competitive intensity also factor in

### 4.3 SDE Calculation

QLB uses **Seller's Discretionary Earnings (SDE):** net profit plus owner salary, personal expenses run through the business, and one-time costs. Advisors guide sellers on what's acceptable to include without alienating buyers.

### 4.4 Pricing Strategy

Advisors typically recommend listing prices **10–15% above expected sale prices** to leave negotiation room. QLB maintains proprietary databases of closed deals by niche, size, and performance metrics, which advisors reference when pricing new listings. The goal is to generate multiple offers within 60 days, creating competitive tension that lifts final prices.

### 4.5 Revenue-Based Multiples

For revenue-based valuations:
- Content sites: 2.8x revenue
- SaaS: 5.2x ARR
- ECommerce: 2.1x revenue

## 5. Seller Experience

### 5.1 Seller Journey

The selling process typically spans **3–6 months** from initial contact to completed transaction:

**Phase 1: Initial Consultation (No Obligation)**
- Confidential conversation with an advisor
- Two-way evaluation of business model, revenue history, growth trajectory, operational complexity, and reasons for selling
- Many sellers have this conversation 6–12 months before listing
- Free, no commitment

**Phase 2: Business Evaluation (Four Pillars)**
- Thorough assessment across Growth, Risk, Transferability, and Documentation
- Specific recommendations on what to address before listing
- Some sellers spend weeks or months improving scores

**Phase 3: Listing Preparation**
- Advisor crafts the listing narrative
- Detailed Confidential Information Memorandum (CIM) prepared
- Listing price set collaboratively based on comparable transactions

**Phase 4: Confidential Marketing**
- Listing shared with database of qualified buyers
- Advisor proactively reaches out to interested buyers
- No public listing—confidentiality protected

**Phase 5: Buyer Qualification and Introductions**
- Advisor screens buyers for financial qualification and genuine intent
- Information flow managed by advisor
- Data room access provided to serious parties

**Phase 6: Negotiations and LOI**
- Advisor acts as intermediary
- Letter of Intent reviewed and advised upon

**Phase 7: Due Diligence and Close**
- Advisor coordinates due diligence process
- Asset Purchase Agreement executed
- Funds move through escrow
- 30–90 day transition period with seller support

### 5.2 Seller Support

- **Pre-listing preparation:** Weeks or months of guidance on improving Documentation and Transferability scores
- **Listing creation:** Professional CIM and listing copy crafted by advisor
- **Buyer screening:** All buyers pre-qualified before introduction
- **Negotiation support:** Advisor buffers emotional friction and advises on offers
- **Post-sale transition:** 30–90 days of seller support facilitated by advisor

### 5.3 Seller Testimonials

- *"Working with QLB in advance on the listing paid off big time because we had three offers within the first week."* — Syed Balkhi (OptinMonster)
- *"As a first-time seller, I was very apprehensive. Now that the deal is done, I can't imagine having dealt with anyone else."* — Eric Dahle
- *"I've been a buyer and a seller through Quiet Light. They're unbiased and the breadth of knowledge they bring is bar none."* — Nathan Singh

### 5.4 Seller Drawbacks

- **Exclusivity agreement:** Binding until the site is sold or withdrawn; cannot sell through other channels
- **3–6 month timeline:** Not suitable for sellers needing rapid liquidity
- **Rejection risk:** 85–90% of applicants are turned away with limited feedback
- **Commission cost:** 10–15% is substantial on smaller deals
- **Advisor assignment:** Cannot choose your advisor; assignment based on availability and niche fit

## 6. Buyer Experience

### 6.1 Buyer Journey

**Step 1: Browse Listings (No NDA Required)**
- Summary financials publicly visible: revenue, profit, multiple, asset type
- Screen dozens of listings without signing anything

**Step 2: Request Full Prospectus**
- Contact the listing advisor directly
- Receive CIM with 3 years of financials, traffic analysis, growth history, and key risk factors (20–50 pages)

**Step 3: Sign NDA and Access Data Room**
- Standard NDA protects seller identity and proprietary data
- Virtual data room (ShareFile or similar) provides secure access
- Seller can track exactly what documents were viewed and by whom

**Step 4: Initial Financial Assessment**
- Review introductory packet with accountant or financial advisor
- 1–2 weeks typical for this phase
- Advisor available for clarification questions

**Step 5: Request Full Due Diligence Package**
- Full bank statements, tax returns, Google Analytics exports, customer/client records, contracts, proprietary data/code

**Step 6: Technical and Operational Due Diligence**
- Third-party technical review recommended for SaaS, eCommerce, and tech-enabled content
- Advisors can recommend vetted vendors
- Most technical audits take 5–10 business days

**Step 7: Submit LOI with Advisor Guidance**
- Advisor helps price offer competitively based on market conditions and comparable closed deals
- Deals typically close within 5–10% of asking price

### 6.2 Buyer Advantages

- **No buyer fees:** $0 to QLB
- **Fully verified listings:** All listings manually verified before publishing
- **Dedicated advisor guidance:** Former operator who has read the financials and knows the seller
- **Transparent financials:** Clean P&L presentation in every data room
- **Confidential access:** Listings not publicly visible, reducing competition
- **Pre-qualified sellers:** All sellers have been vetted, reducing fraud risk
- **Escrow protection:** All transactions route through Escrow.com

### 6.3 Buyer Drawbacks

- **Minimum deal size:** ~$100K (most listings $300K+)
- **Limited inventory:** 5–20 active matching listings at any given time
- **Longer timelines:** 60–90 days standard; SBA financing can extend to 90–120 days
- **NDA bottleneck:** Cannot see full financials until NDA is signed
- **Higher asking prices:** Sophisticated sellers with realistic valuations mean less room for negotiation
- **Less FBA inventory:** Compared to Empire Flippers

## 7. Competitive Advantages

### 7.1 Entrepreneur-Only Advisor Model

QLB's most significant differentiator: every advisor has personally built, bought, or sold at least one online business. This is not a marketing claim—it's a hiring requirement. The practical implications:

- Advisors understand churn normalization, founder dependencies, and MRR inconsistency from firsthand experience
- They can identify structural risks in listings that traditional brokers would miss
- They provide empathy and practical understanding that career dealmakers cannot replicate
- Their reputation depends on placing deals that work out well for both sides

### 7.2 Vetting-First Model

The 85–90% rejection rate creates a **credibility signal** that reduces buyer due diligence friction. When a buyer sees a QLB listing, they know it has passed multiple gates. This scarcity prevents marketplace saturation that depresses multiples on open platforms.

### 7.3 Four Pillars Framework

The proprietary evaluation framework provides:
- **Actionable preparation guidance:** Sellers know exactly what to improve before listing
- **Consistent evaluation standard:** Buyers can trust that all listings have been evaluated against the same criteria
- **Value creation during pre-listing period:** Weeks of preparation translate to higher sale prices

### 7.4 Confidential Marketing Approach

Unlike public marketplaces, QLB's listings are shared privately with qualified buyers. This:
- Protects seller confidentiality (employees, customers, competitors don't know)
- Reduces competition among buyers
- Allows for more candid discussions about business challenges

### 7.5 Track Record and Institutional Knowledge

- Founded in 2006, nearly two decades of completed transactions
- 750+ businesses sold, $500M+ in transaction value
- Deep knowledge of market cycles, buyer behavior, and deal structures
- Axial Top 25 LMM Brokers No. 11 (1H 2026)

### 7.6 Educational Investment

QLB provides extensive free education through its podcast, blog, and Joe Valley's book. Sellers who engage with this content before their consultation are better prepared and typically have smoother transactions.

### 7.7 Escrow Protection

All transactions route through Escrow.com, with funds released only after buyers verify asset transfers. This virtually eliminates scam risks common on open marketplaces like Flippa.

## 8. Bottlenecks and Limitations

### 8.1 Deal Flow Bottleneck

QLB's vetting-first model inherently limits deal flow. By rejecting 85–90% of sellers, the firm maintains quality but restricts growth. This creates a **scalability ceiling**—the model cannot easily scale without either lowering vetting standards (compromising quality) or hiring more advisors (diluting the entrepreneur-only culture).

### 8.2 Time-to-Close Bottleneck

The 3–6 month timeline (60–90 days minimum) is significantly longer than auction-format platforms that can close in days or weeks. This creates friction for:
- Sellers needing rapid liquidity
- Buyers who want to deploy capital quickly
- Time-sensitive transactions (market timing, personal circumstances)

### 8.3 Inventory Bottleneck

With only 5–20 active matching listings at any given time, buyers have limited selection compared to Flippa (1,000+ listings) or Empire Flippers (200+ monthly). This means:
- Buyers may wait months for a matching listing
- Less price competition among sellers
- Potential buyers may be lost to platforms with more inventory

### 8.4 NDA Bottleneck

The NDA requirement creates friction in the buyer journey. Buyers cannot see full financials until they sign an NDA, which:
- Requires a time investment before knowing if a deal is worth pursuing
- Can result in 5–6 NDA processes for every actual acquisition
- Demands a highly organized workflow to manage parallel negotiations

### 8.5 Advisor Assignment Bottleneck

Sellers cannot choose their advisor. Assignment is based on availability and niche fit. This creates risk of:
- Mismatched advisor-seller pairings
- Inconsistent experience quality
- No mechanism to switch advisors mid-process

### 8.6 Exclusivity Bottleneck

The binding exclusivity agreement means:
- Sellers cannot list on other platforms simultaneously
- If the listing doesn't result in a sale, months of market time are lost
- Sellers cannot test multiple channels to gauge market interest

### 8.7 Geographic and Category Limitations

- Deeper expertise in US-based businesses; fewer reference points for international acquisitions
- Conservative on emerging categories and new platforms
- Limited inventory in certain verticals (e.g., fewer FBA deals than Empire Flippers)

### 8.8 Commission Bottleneck on Small Deals

On deals under $200K, the 10–15% commission represents a substantial portion of the sale price. For a $150K sale at 15% commission, the seller pays $22,500—potentially months of profit. This makes QLB economically unattractive for smaller businesses.

## 9. NP-Hard Problems and Computational Complexity

### 9.1 The Matching Problem

At its core, QLB's business model involves matching sellers with buyers—a variant of the **stable marriage problem** or **assignment problem**. Given:
- A set of sellers with businesses characterized by multiple attributes (niche, size, growth, risk, transferability, documentation)
- A set of buyers with preferences over those attributes
- A set of constraints (deal size, timeline, financing, geographic preferences)

Finding the optimal matching that maximizes total welfare (or QLB's commission revenue) is computationally challenging. While the basic assignment problem is solvable in polynomial time (O(n³) via the Hungarian algorithm), the real-world version with:
- Multi-dimensional preferences
- Incomplete information (buyers don't see all attributes until NDA)
- Dynamic arrivals (new listings and buyers over time)
- Strategic behavior (sellers and buyers may misrepresent preferences)

...makes the problem significantly harder. The online version of this problem, where decisions must be made without knowledge of future arrivals, is known to be competitive-ratio bounded but not optimally solvable.

### 9.2 Valuation as a Multi-Objective Optimization

QLB's Four Pillars framework requires evaluating businesses across four dimensions simultaneously. This is a **multi-objective optimization problem** where:
- Growth, Risk, Transferability, and Documentation may conflict
- There is no single "optimal" business—only Pareto-optimal trade-offs
- The weighting of pillars varies by buyer type (aggregators vs. operators vs. investors)

Multi-objective optimization is generally NP-hard when objectives conflict, as finding the Pareto front can require exponential computation in the worst case.

### 9.3 Deal Structuring as a Constraint Satisfaction Problem

Structuring a deal involves satisfying multiple constraints simultaneously:
- Price within buyer's budget
- Transition period acceptable to both parties
- Financing structure (cash vs. SBA vs. seller financing)
- Escrow terms
- Non-compete agreements
- Intellectual property transfer

This is a **constraint satisfaction problem (CSP)**, which is NP-complete in general. While individual deals can be structured heuristically, finding the globally optimal deal structure that maximizes joint utility is computationally intractable for complex deals.

### 9.4 Buyer Screening as a Classification Problem

QLB's vetting process is essentially a **binary classification problem**: accept or reject a seller based on features (revenue consistency, margins, traffic diversification, documentation quality, transferability). The challenge is:
- **Imbalanced data:** Only 10–15% of applicants are accepted
- **Concept drift:** Market conditions change what constitutes a "good" business
- **Adversarial behavior:** Sellers may attempt to game the vetting process
- **High cost of false positives:** Listing a bad business damages QLB's reputation

Training a classifier that minimizes both false positives and false negatives in an adversarial, non-stationary environment is a well-known hard problem in machine learning.

### 9.5 Portfolio Optimization for Buyers

For aggregator buyers (e.g., Thrasio-model companies) building portfolios of online businesses, the problem of selecting which businesses to acquire is a **portfolio optimization problem**:
- Maximize expected return (profit) subject to risk constraints
- Account for correlations between businesses (e.g., two content sites dependent on Google algorithm updates)
- Budget constraints (total capital available)
- Operational capacity constraints (how many businesses can be managed)

This is a variant of the **knapsack problem** with additional constraints, which is NP-hard. The cardinality-constrained portfolio optimization problem is known to be NP-hard even with simplifying assumptions.

### 9.6 Scheduling and Resource Allocation

With 8–18 advisors managing 100–150 deals per year, QLB faces a **resource allocation problem**:
- Assign advisors to deals based on niche expertise and availability
- Schedule buyer introductions, due diligence phases, and closing timelines
- Manage parallel deals without overloading any single advisor

This is a **job shop scheduling problem**, which is NP-hard. The dynamic version, where new deals arrive and timelines shift unpredictably, is even harder.

### 9.7 Market Design and Mechanism Design

QLB's entire business model is a **mechanism design** problem: designing rules and incentives such that:
- Sellers truthfully disclose financial information
- Buyers are incentivized to close deals rather than walk away
- Advisors are motivated to maximize deal value rather than just closing
- The platform extracts sufficient commission to sustain operations

Mechanism design with private information, multiple agents, and dynamic interactions is a frontier area of research with many open problems. The **revenue-maximizing auction design** problem, for instance, is solved only for restricted settings.
