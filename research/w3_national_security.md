# W3: National Security Screening for Technology Acquisitions

## Executive Summary

National security screening of technology acquisitions has become a critical, multi-jurisdictional process affecting cross-border M&A, foreign direct investment (FDI), and defense technology transfer. This research synthesizes findings from 10 web searches covering CFIUS, FDI screening mechanisms, valuation impacts, computational bottlenecks, and optimization approaches. The screening landscape is characterized by expanding jurisdictional scope, increasing complexity in TID (Technology, Infrastructure, Data) business classification, significant valuation effects, and growing computational challenges in risk assessment and resource allocation.

---

## 1. Screening Process

### 1.1 CFIUS Review Process

The Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States (CFIUS) is an interagency committee chaired by the Secretary of the Treasury, comprising nine Cabinet members including Secretaries of State, Defense, Homeland Security, Commerce, and Energy, the Attorney General, U.S. Trade Representative, and Director of the Office of Science and Technology Policy.

**Jurisdiction covers:**
- Mergers, acquisitions, and takeovers resulting in foreign control of a U.S. business
- Certain noncontrolling investments in TID U.S. businesses (Technology, Infrastructure, Data)
- Certain real estate transactions
- Critical technologies subject to export licensing/controls

**Review phases:**
1. **National Security Review** (initial 45-day period)
2. **National Security Investigation** (additional 45 days if threats identified)
3. **Presidential Decision** (15-day period)

**Mandatory filing** is required when:
- A foreign government acquires a "substantial interest" in a TID U.S. business
- A transaction involves a TID U.S. business producing critical technology subject to export controls

**Key statutory factors** (Section 721, as amended by FIRRMA 2018):
- Domestic production needed for national defense
- Control of domestic industries by foreign citizens
- Effects on military goods sales to terrorism-supporting countries
- U.S. technological leadership in national security areas
- Critical infrastructure and critical technology effects
- Supply chain resilience (E.O. 14083, 2022)
- Cybersecurity risks
- Sensitive personal data access

### 1.2 Multi-Jurisdictional Screening

National security screening extends beyond CFIUS to multiple jurisdictions:

| Jurisdiction | Regime | Key Features |
|-------------|--------|--------------|
| United States | CFIUS | TID businesses, mandatory filing for foreign government investments |
| France | IEF (Investissements Étrangers en France) | Sensitive activity analysis beyond defense: energy, AI, robotics, semiconductors, data infrastructure |
| United Kingdom | NSIA (National Security and Investment Act) | 17 sensitive sectors, mandatory notification, not nationality-based |
| Poland | Act on Control of Certain Investments | Sector-specific thresholds |
| Italy | Golden Power | Veto in defense/national security cases |
| EU | FDI Screening Regulation | Cooperation mechanism, common minimum scope, 22 member states with mechanisms |

### 1.3 Due Diligence Framework

**DNI Due Diligence Guidance** recommends:
- Verifying identity of prospective partners
- Checking for unwanted control or influence
- Confirming motive alignment
- Assessing IP risk exposure
- Reviewing ownership chain to ultimate beneficial owner (UBO)
- Screening against sanctions lists and entity lists
- Checking for PEP (Politically Exposed Person) associations
- Identifying foreign military contracts

**Australian Investee Due Diligence Guidance** adds:
- Pre-investment considerations
- FOCI (Foreign Ownership, Control, or Influence) risk assessment
- Indicators of Concern (IoC) framework
- Post-investment monitoring with trigger-based reviews
- Event-based reviews for ownership changes and geopolitical shifts

## 2. CFIUS

### 2.1 Evolution and Expansion

- **2007**: Original concerns over Japanese acquisitions of U.S. defense firms
- **2018**: FIRRMA (Foreign Investment Risk Review Modernization Act) expanded jurisdiction to noncontrolling investments in TID businesses
- **2020**: Trump ordered ByteDance to divest from musical.ly (TikTok)
- **2022**: E.O. 14083 expanded factors to include supply chain resilience, cybersecurity, and sensitive data
- **2024**: Biden ordered PRC cryptocurrency mining firm to divest real estate near strategic missile base
- **2025-2026**: NSSTS signals CFIUS scope expansion to greenfield investments and broader critical technology jurisdiction

### 2.2 TID Business Classification

TID businesses are categorized by:
1. **Critical Technologies**: Emerging and foundational technologies subject to export controls (Export Control Reform Act of 2018)
2. **Critical Infrastructure**: 28 specified subsectors
3. **Sensitive Personal Data**: Data that can be used for surveillance, tracking, or targeting

### 2.3 Mitigation Agreements

CFIUS can impose structural and behavioral mitigation measures:
- Data governance protocols
- Personnel access restrictions
- Domestic sourcing requirements
- Ongoing audit and reporting obligations
- Security officer appointments
- Third-party monitoring

## 3. FDI Screening

### 3.1 EU FDI Screening Framework

The EU FDI Screening Regulation (in force since October 2020) established:
- Cooperation mechanism between Member States and European Commission
- Common minimum scope: dual-use items, military equipment, critical raw materials, AI, energy, transport, digital infrastructure
- Screening of investments through EU-based subsidiaries
- Information exchange tools to prevent circumvention

**Key statistics (2022):**
- 420+ FDI cases screened
- 87% assessed within 15 calendar days
- Less than 3% led to Commission opinion
- Top sources: US, UK, China, Japan, Cayman Islands, Canada
- 59% manufacturing (energy, aerospace, defense, semiconductors, health, data processing, communication, transport, cybersecurity)

### 3.2 2026 Revision

The revised EU framework (adopted June 2026):
- Requires all member states to establish screening mechanisms
- Extends common minimum scope to sensitive sectors, technologies, and infrastructure
- Maintains national responsibility for screening decisions
- Improves cooperation and information exchange
- Streamlines procedures for investors and authorities

### 3.3 Global Trends

- 22 EU Member States now have national screening mechanisms (up from 11 in 2020)
- Growing convergence on critical technology definitions
- Increasing coordination with allies (FIRRMA-mandated information sharing)
- Trend toward mandatory notification regimes

## 4. Valuation Impact

### 4.1 Direct Valuation Effects

**Stock market reactions:**
- U.S. target firm stock prices inversely related to ex-ante probability of CFIUS failure
- Significant spillover valuation effects on industry peers
- Market prices in regulatory risk at announcement

**Transaction structuring impacts:**
- Reverse termination fees: 2-5% of transaction value
- CFIUS-related costs: $200,000-$500,000+ per transaction
- Timeline uncertainty reduces buyer valuations
- Mitigation obligations affect target company value and attractiveness

### 4.2 Capital Flow Effects

- Tightening of national security reviews drives capital flows from China away from the U.S.
- Increased offers from U.S. allies creating fragmented market along geopolitical lines
- CFIUS interventions adversely affect productivity of critical industry firms in the U.S.
- Foreign buyers may offer premium valuations for technology assets, but regulatory risk discounts offset premiums

### 4.3 Deal Structuring Considerations

- Voluntary filing before governmental inquiry provides stronger mitigation leverage
- Rights packages calibrated to genuine needs reduce CFIUS sensitivity
- Timelines accommodating regulatory review prevent breach risk
- Representations addressing national security landscape of target business
- Early assessment of regulatory exposure before signing LOI

## 5. Bottlenecks

### 5.1 Process Bottlenecks

**CFIUS review bottlenecks:**
- Expanding jurisdiction creating uncertainty
- Complex TID business classification
- Interagency coordination delays
- Mitigation negotiation timelines
- Presidential review uncertainty

**Security clearance bottlenecks:**
- Facility Security Clearance (FCL) timelines: 2+ years traditionally
- CLASS ACT program (2026) addresses pre-contract clearance for nontraditional contractors
- Clearance timelines: Secret 1-6 months, TS 4-12 months, TS/SCI 6-18 months
- Polygraph waitlists: 14-22 months for TS/SCI with full-scope polygraph
- DCSA processing: 227 days (TS), 156 days (Secret) for fastest 90%

### 5.2 Operational Bottlenecks

**Screening system bottlenecks:**
- Airport security checkpoint congestion
- Power infrastructure failures causing compounding delays
- Single-point-of-failure in screening equipment
- Queueing theory challenges: M/M/1 vs M/M/c models
- Throughput vs. security trade-off

### 5.3 Information Bottlenecks

- Beneficial ownership transparency gaps
- Cross-jurisdictional information sharing limitations
- Classification of emerging technologies
- Real-time threat intelligence integration
- Interagency data synchronization

## 6. NP-Hard Problems

### 6.1 Computational Complexity in Screening

While no direct research identifies CFIUS screening as NP-hard, several related optimization problems in national security screening are computationally challenging:

**Screening Resource Allocation:**
- Multi-level security screening allocation (McLay et al., 2006, 2010)
- Passenger-to-security-class assignment as Markov decision process
- Integer optimization programs for threat-based screening
- Budget-constrained screening with waiting time constraints

**Risk Assessment Complexity:**
- Combinatorial explosion in ownership chain analysis
- Multi-jurisdictional compliance optimization
- Real-time threat scoring with incomplete information
- Supply chain risk propagation

### 6.2 Optimization Approaches

**Simulation optimization:**
- Trade-off between security benefit and congestion impact
- Risk-based screening with threat values
- Queueing theory models for throughput optimization
- Multi-server parallel processing models

**AI/ML approaches:**
- Foundation models for non-intrusive screening
- Automated threat detection from sensor data
- Dynamic risk profiling
- Predictive analytics for clearance adjudication

### 6.3 Heuristic Solutions

For NP-hard screening problems, practical approaches include:
- Heuristic algorithms for resource allocation
- Greedy approaches for threat-based prioritization
- Approximation algorithms for multi-objective optimization
- Machine learning for pattern recognition in screening data

## 8. Key Findings Summary

| Dimension | Key Finding | Implication |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| **Screening Process** | Multi-jurisdictional, expanding scope | Requires early assessment in deal planning |
| **CFIUS** | TID classification, mandatory filing, mitigation agreements | Structural deal terms must address CFIUS risk |
| **FDI** | 22 EU mechanisms, 420+ cases/year, <3% opinion rate | Low intervention rate but high uncertainty |
| **Valuation** | 2-5% reverse termination fees, $200K-$500K costs | Regulatory risk directly affects pricing |
| **Bottlenecks** | Clearance timelines 6-22 months, interagency delays | Timeline planning critical for deal certainty |
| **NP-Hard** | Resource allocation, risk scoring, multi-objective optimization | Heuristic and AI/ML approaches necessary |
| **Technology Transfer** | Export controls, FOCI risk, APFIT acceleration | Dual-track: control and accelerate |
